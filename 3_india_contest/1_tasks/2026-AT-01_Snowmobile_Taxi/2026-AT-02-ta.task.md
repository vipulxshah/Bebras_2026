![body] 6 நாணங்கள் உள்ளன. அவற்றின் மீது 1 முதல் 6 வரை எண்கள் எழுதப்பட்டுள்ளன. மேலும் 6 துளைகள் உள்ளன. அவையும் 1 முதல் 6 வரை எண்கள் கொண்டவை.

A. ஆரம்பத்தில், நாணங்கள் சீரற்ற முறையில் துளைகளில் வைக்கப்பட்டிருக்கும். எல்லா நாணங்களும் அவற்றின் சரியான துளைகளுக்கு போக, கீழே சொல்லப்பட்ட செயல்முறையை செய்கிறோம்.

B. மேலிருந்து கீழாகப் பார்த்து, தவறாக இருக்கும் முதல் நாணத்தை எடுக்க வேண்டும். அந்த நாணத்தை, அதன் எண்ணுக்கு ஒத்த துளையின் வலதுபுறத்தில் வைக்க வேண்டும்.

இது, நாணம் எடுத்துச் சென்ற துளையை காலியாக்கும்.

C. அந்த காலியிடத்தில், அந்த துளையின் எண்ணுக்கு ஒத்த நாணத்தை வைக்க வேண்டும்.

D. இதனால் இன்னொரு துளை காலியாகும். அந்த காலியிடத்திலும், அங்கு இருக்க வேண்டிய நாணத்தை போட்டு நிரப்ப வேண்டும்.

இது தொடர்ந்தும் நடந்து, நாணம் வைக்கப்பட்ட வலதுபுற துளைக்கு அடுத்த துளை காலியாகும் வரை நடக்கும்.

E. பக்கத்தில் இருந்த நாணம், அந்த காலியிடத்தில் போடப்பட்டு, எல்லா துளைகளும் மீண்டும் நிரம்பும்.

இப்படி நடந்த எல்லா அசைவுகளின் தொடரை (A-E) ஒரு சுழற்சி (one turn) என்று சொல்கிறோம்.

F. பிறகு இதே செயல்முறையை இன்னொரு சுழற்சியாக மீண்டும் செய்யலாம். எல்லா நாணங்களும் சரியான துளைகளுக்கு வரும்வரை இதை தொடரலாம்.

## Answer Explanation


The correct answer is: 3) 1, 3

![explain1]

The example above shows that a solution is possible in one turn. 

Upper bound:

Let’s define a cycle as a group of coins where each coin is sitting in the place of another coin from the same group, forming a loop.

For example, a cycle of 3 coins could be:
- Coin 1 is in slot 2
- Coin 2 is in slot 3
- Coin 3 is in slot 1

Each turn fixes one whole cycle.

Non-trivial cycles (i.e. cycles of misplaced coins) contain at least 2 elements, since a correctly placed coin forms a trivial cycle of length 1 and is not part of a cycle of misplacements 

So with 6 coins, the largest possible number of cycles is:

6÷2=3

For example:

1→2→1,3→4→3,5→6→5

Therefore, for 6 coins, at most 3 turns are required.

## This is Informatics

The procedure in this task is a sorting method. Sorting means arranging data so that everything ends up in the correct position. Computers do this very often, for example when ordering numbers, organizing files, or managing data in memory.

In this problem, each coin already indicates where it belongs: the number on the coin corresponds to the compartment with the same number. The goal is therefore to arrange the coins in the order 1, 2, 3, …. Sorting numbers into this fixed order is a special case of the general sorting problem, where the correct position of an element is not known beforehand and must be determined during the sorting process.

The method follows a clear algorithm: a precise sequence of steps that can be executed systematically until the correct arrangement is reached.

Another interesting aspect is that the method requires almost no additional memory. Apart from a single temporary place (the coin placed next to a compartment), all movements happen directly within the existing compartments. In computer science, such algorithms are called in-place algorithms, meaning they rearrange the data without needing extra storage.

How many turns are needed also reflects another important activity in computer science: analyzing algorithms. Computer scientists often investigate how many steps an algorithm needs in the best case and in the worst case.

Cost

The cost of the algorithm can be measured by counting the number of coin movements. Each coin is moved exactly once during the overall process, except that in each turn the first coin is moved twice (once when it is taken out and once when it is placed back).

If there are (n) coins and the process requires (m) turns, the total number of movements is therefore: n + m
In the worst case, each turn places only two coins into their correct slots, so at most (n/2) turns are needed. This gives a maximum of n + n / 2 = 3/2·n movements.

Since constant factors are ignored in Big-O notation, the cost of the algorithm is O(n).

## This is Computational Thinking

First, the problem encourages abstraction. Instead of focusing on coins and compartments, we can think of the situation more generally as objects that must be moved to their correct positions. This abstraction helps us understand the underlying structure of the problem.

Second, the task involves algorithmic thinking. The rules describe a precise step-by-step procedure: find the first misplaced coin, move it next to its correct compartment, and repeatedly fill the empty compartment with the correct coin until the turn is completed. Thinking in terms of such clear procedures is a central skill in computational thinking.

The task also demonstrates decomposition, where a complex problem is broken down into smaller parts. For example, determining the shortest possible move is one subtask. Using this observation to deduce the maximum number of moves required is another. By solving these smaller subtasks, the overall problem becomes easier to understand and solve.
